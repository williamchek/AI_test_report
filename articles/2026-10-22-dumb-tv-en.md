# The People Making Their TVs Dumb

> As televisions start watching you, a crowd is using AI to dismantle the watching. A revolt over who owns the thing you bought.

On the afternoon of October 17, a developer named Gathin T. sat in his parents' living room and tried to open Netflix for them. The TV played a sponsored ad first. He fed the TV's model number to Claude Code; the AI found the Xiaomi set over Wi-Fi, removed 25 preinstalled packages, and wrote a brand-new launcher. "Looks like an Apple TV now lol," he posted on X, with before-and-after photos.

The post was viewed 7.4 million times.

Over the next two days, thousands of people did the same thing to their own TVs. Two open-source projects exploded on GitHub: Gathin's CleanYourTV (originally tv-vibehack) and Muzzammil Yasin Sabuwala's dumbtv. Some called that weekend the "smart TV uprising."

But this is more than a geek's weekend toy. Over the past decade, the television has quietly become the most surveilling device in the home: it identifies what you watch — including the game console and laptop you plug in over HDMI — and sells that data to advertisers. Manufacturers now earn so much from your attention that it subsidizes the price of the hardware. And AI has just demolished the barricade to resistance: what used to take rooting, flashing, and several days and nights now takes one sentence to an AI and fifteen minutes.

This piece is about three things: how your TV watches you, how much money TV makers earn from your gaze, and how far this revolt can go — and where it will crash.

## Your TV Is Watching You

The surveillance system has a name: ACR, automatic content recognition. Think of it as Shazam for your screen — the TV periodically captures frames or audio, turns them into a perceptual hash on-device, and sends only the hash to a server to match against a content database. No raw screenshots leave the set, but it's enough to know precisely which channel, which episode, which ad you're watching.

Three numbers belong to three different layers; don't mix them. The Texas attorney general's complaint, citing Samsung's internal documents, alleges Samsung captures the screen "every 500 milliseconds" — that's the on-device collection rate, and it's the plaintiff's allegation, not a court finding. A 2024 peer-reviewed paper actually measured the network traffic: roughly one upload every 60 seconds for Samsung, every 15 seconds for LG. Collection is one thing; transmission is another.

More important: ACR doesn't only watch the TV's own apps. The paper confirmed that ACR traffic flows even when the TV is used as an external display — a laptop, game console, or set-top box over HDMI. And the feature is on by default; turning it off takes digging through six or seven layers of menus. "The average user is unlikely to know what ACR is or that it can opt out," says Anna Maria Mandalari, a researcher at University College London.

This isn't news. It's history. In 2017, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission settled with Vizio: starting in 2014, Vizio had enabled its subsidiary's ACR software by default on 11 million TVs, logging viewing second by second, transmitting it with IP addresses, and matching it through data brokers to demographics — gender, age, income, marital status — for sale to third parties for ad targeting. The fine was $2.2 million, and data collected before March 2016 had to be deleted. That's all in court documents.

History repeated on December 15, 2025, at larger scale. Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton sued five manufacturers at once — Sony, Samsung, LG, Hisense, TCL — accusing them of collecting viewing data without meaningful consent and selling it to advertisers, seeking up to $10,000 per violation and up to $250,000 per violation involving consumers 65 or older. "Companies, especially those connected to the Chinese Communist Party, have no business illegally recording Americans' devices inside their own homes," Paxton said in the announcement. "This conduct is invasive, deceptive, and unlawful."

As of October 2026, Samsung (February) and LG (May) have settled, agreeing to clearer consent screens; Hisense was ordered not to collect Texas users' data while its case proceeds; the Sony and TCL cases are ongoing. An honest note: these are the plaintiff's allegations, and a settlement is not an admission of guilt.

## An Ad Company That Happens to Sell TVs

Why would manufacturers take such risks? Because the business is enormous.

LG's own press release puts it bluntly: advertising and services revenue on webOS was expected to exceed KRW 1 trillion (about $690 million) in 2024 — four times the 2021 figure. Roku has gone further — it's barely a streaming-stick company anymore, with 2025 platform revenue (advertising plus distribution) at $4.15 billion, up 18%. "There's still a lot of ad dollars that is in the traditional linear ecosystem that's still moving to streaming," CEO Anthony Wood said on the earnings call. "We're taking, I would say, more than our fair share of those dollars."

The most ironic case is Vizio. After paying the $2.2 million fine, it was acquired by Walmart in 2024 for about $2.3 billion — with its advertising business part of the attraction. Before the acquisition, Vizio's average revenue per user had doubled from $10.44 to $19.89 (per its 2022 earnings call), 77% of it directly from advertising.

Honesty requires leaving room for the other side: the ad subsidy is real consumer welfare. Without that revenue, the same panel would cost more. Part of what you paid for that screen was paid with your attention. Which is also the hardest question this revolt has to answer: when you rip out the ads, you're also ripping out the hand that made the TV cheap.

## The Fifteen-Minute Revolt

Back to that weekend. What Gathin did isn't technically mysterious. What's mysterious is how simple AI made it.

Both projects run the same three steps: enable ADB in the TV's developer options (any computer on the same Wi-Fi can then reach the TV), run `pm disable-user` to *disable* — note, disable, not delete — the manufacturer's telemetry, ad services, content rows, and preinstalled apps one by one, and install a lightweight launcher as the default home screen. Back up the package list; one command restores everything. CleanYourTV's FAQ is explicit: "Nothing is flashed, rooted or deleted; packages are disabled in the user profile."

dumbtv goes further into engineering. Its README reads like a construction manual written for AI agents: a complete shell-script pipeline, a device compatibility catalog, a machine-readable AGENTS.md contract, even a SKILL.md for agents. It ships a 45KB zero-permission launcher and an on-device DNS filter that resolves Amazon telemetry, Alexa endpoints, and OTA servers to 127.0.0.1 — ad connections die on the TV itself, while YouTube and the app store keep working.

And AI changed the threshold. Manual TV debloating predates all this — the open-source Universal Android Debloater for phones has been around for years, and Turkish developer Mert Cobanov published a one-page guide with a ready-made prompt back in August 2026. The difference is speed: days to weeks of manual work became paste-the-PROMPT.md, answer a few questions, fifteen minutes done. In Gathin's words from his fundraising page: "I put the prompt on GitHub, tweeted the before and after, and a few thousand people did the same to their own TVs over the next two days."

AI isn't frictionless, though. Claude's safety filters flatly refuse a naked "hack my TV" request; users have to paste the repo's entire prompt to get around it. The AI will help you, but first you have to prove you know what you're doing.

## An Honest Boundary: The Top Two Brands Are Untouchable

This is the most important honest moment in the piece: this "uprising" cannot touch the world's top two TV brands.

Samsung runs Tizen, LG runs webOS. Neither has ADB or `pm disable-user`; dumbtv's docs list Samsung, LG, Roku, VIDAA, Vizio, and Panasonic as "no shell, no path" — no way in. What *can* be touched is Android TV / Google TV (Sony, TCL, Xiaomi, Philips, Hisense, and others) and Fire TV (debloat only — Fire OS won't hand the home screen to third-party launchers).

Per TrendForce's Q3 2025 figures, Samsung holds 17.2% of global shipments and LG 11.7% — precisely the top two. However loud this revolt gets, nearly 30% of TVs were never on the battlefield to begin with. The original pitch claimed the trend spanned LG. That was wrong — corrected here.

## The Crash Logs

"Disabled, not deleted" sounds safe, but the devil lives in firmware. CleanYourTV's issues section is a crash log worth reading; two entries stand out.

The first was a false alarm. An aging COOP TV (Android 4.4.2) froze on the boot screen after 17 packages were disabled; for a while it looked like ADB was dead too. It turned out to be a loose network cable. The reporter re-enabled all 58 backed-up packages with `pm enable`, the TV came back to life, and a careful four-batch redo safely disabled 13 of 17 packages. Its value is as proof that the backup-and-restore mechanism actually saves lives — and that old firmware is a high-risk zone.

The second is the most technically valuable user report in the repo. A mainland-China Skyworth 8R40_K5C hid three traps in its firmware, costing the reporter two factory resets in one night: the ADB toggle lives behind the "About" page, which depends on packages that — once disabled — make ADB permanently unreachable; no third-party launcher can claim the home screen because boot-time settings in a read-only partition overwrite it (force it and you get a black screen); and the ad server address (`hoisin.coocaatv.com`) is baked into the read-only partition, phoning home 5 seconds after boot, with a 15-second boot ad video sitting at `/data/local/bootvideo.ts`. The reporter ended up with a reboot-verified list of 16 safely removable packages — with a Chinese-language summary attached.

dumbtv's docs say the ugly part up front: it hard-codes a never-disable list — launcher, settings, TV input routing, Bluetooth pairing, system UI — "disable these and it's a black screen plus a dead remote"; `/system` is dm-verity protected, and tampering is "a documented path to bricking." Its GOTCHAS.md opens with: "Everything here cost real time to work out. Read it before debugging anything."

Some questions have no answers yet: no manufacturer has said anything about whether this voids warranties; ADB runs in plaintext on port 5555, so in theory any device on the same LAN can get a shell — close it and revoke authorizations when done; and the "bricked TV" cases mentioned in press coverage have no named, unrecoverable instance in either repo's issues — everything found was eventually recovered. That doesn't mean it can't happen. It means there's currently no evidence.

## Can Taiwan's TVs Join In?

The brands on Taiwanese shelves: Samsung, LG, Sony, TCL, Xiaomi, Philips, Panasonic, Toshiba, plus local names like BenQ, CHIMEI, HERAN, and SAMPO. Much of what TCL, Xiaomi, Sony, BenQ, and Philips sell is Google TV / Android TV — exactly the platform this wave can touch. Samsung and LG owners are entirely outside it.

Two honest caveats. First, "Taiwan-sold models on the same OS have ACR too" is an inference from platform consistency, not an independent technical verification of Taiwanese units. Second, Taiwan currently has no enforcement or penalty cases over smart TV viewing-data collection — the U.S. has the Texas lawsuit; Taiwan's column is blank. Both points are in the article so readers don't assume someone is already minding the store.

## What Taiwan Thinks: Annoyed by Ads, Not Yet Thinking About Data

While writing this, I searched Taiwan's public conversation. The result was near-silence. No ACR discussion has formed on PTT, Dcard, or Mobile01; Taiwan's mainstream media has all but collectively skipped the Texas lawsuit — the only mention found in this search was from the crypto outlet BlockTempo, which correctly noted "these are the prosecutor's allegations, not yet adjudicated." The Consumers' Foundation has said nothing, published no complaint statistics, and no Taiwanese poll on smart TV privacy exists.

That doesn't mean Taiwanese consumers don't care — it means the caring sits at a different layer. A TV recommendation post on Dcard (which discloses affiliate links at the end) lists "no ads on boot" as a must-have feature of LG's webOS; a Mobile01 user wrote in an ad-complaint thread that "on Taiwan's side there's even less that can regulate this." Hating ads is real. But "the TV is selling your viewing records" hasn't entered most people's frame. My read: on "the TV is watching you," Taiwan is still at the "ads are so annoying" sensory layer, not yet at the "data is being sold" cognitive layer. (That's an observation, not a conclusion.)

Two local signals are worth noting. First, the tech outlet life.tw reported that CleanYourTV's community list already includes Taiwan's BenQ E55-735 — at least one Taiwanese user has actually done it, filing details like a Chromecast remote failing to wake from deep standby. The uprising isn't theoretical in Taiwan. Second, LG Taiwan issued an official statement on September 10, 2026, saying LG smart displays sold in Taiwan "have no ACR functionality." That's the manufacturer's own claim, never independently verified, and it covers only LG — Samsung, Sony, TCL, and Xiaomi models sold in Taiwan are outside the statement. Both sides on the table: some infer Taiwanese models have it too (platform consistency); LG says its own don't (unverified). Nobody has independently measured.

The Chinese-language tech blog shrimp-pool put it precisely: "'Removing ads' and 'stopping tracking' are not the same thing." Even with a clean home screen, the system underneath may still be phoning home. Taiwanese readers should keep that sentence.

## Suing in Taiwan: What You'd Win Isn't Money — It's the Consent Screen

So could you sue in Taiwan? Start with the substantive law: there are three arrows in the quiver.

The strongest is Article 20 of the Personal Data Protection Act — use beyond the original purpose. A manufacturer collecting viewing data can stretch to claim it's "providing services"; selling that data to brokers who resell it to advertisers plainly exceeds the "specific purpose" any buyer understood. Beyond-purpose use needs your consent — and Article 7(2) requires that consent to be "separately expressed after the collector has explicitly informed the individual of the other purposes of use beyond the specific purpose, their scope, and the effect of consenting or not on their rights and interests." Note the word *separately*: a bundled checkbox buried in a 50,000-word privacy policy almost certainly fails. The Chinese-language tech outlet Uncle Winston counted LG's setup flow at 10 agreements and 50,000 words — three to four hours of reading (a single-source claim, for reference only). That kind of "notice" is hard to call explicit.

The second arrow is Articles 8 and 9, the notice obligations. ACR on by default, with the off switch buried six or seven menus deep, is hard to square with "explicitly informing" users of the statutory items like purpose and recipients. And Article 7(4) puts the burden of proof on the collector: the manufacturer must prove the user actually consented — not the other way around.

The third arrow is Article 2: a viewing fingerprint combined with an IP address, device ID, or account is "data by which a person can be directly or indirectly identified" — personal data. In the Vizio case, TVs transmitted second-by-second viewing plus IP addresses, which brokers then joined to demographics. Indirect identification is barely contestable.

The arrows exist. But three walls stand in front of them.

The first is evidence. Proving ACR actually transmits requires packet capture — the kind of thing security researchers do in lab conditions. Proving the data was sold to brokers is worse: Taiwan has no U.S.-style discovery, and those contracts sit in the manufacturer's filing cabinets. The second wall is the regulator: the Personal Data Protection Commission still doesn't formally exist — only a preparatory office set up in December 2023, with no fining power. Amendments passed in November 2025 designate the commission as the competent authority, but their effective date has never been announced. Enforcement today is scattered across industry regulators; TVs presumably fall to the Ministry of Economic Affairs, whose appetite for investigating multinational TV makers is unknown.

The third wall is the most practical: breaking the law is cheap. Under Article 47, violating Articles 19 or 20 draws a NT$50,000–500,000 fine *plus an order to correct within a deadline*; violating the notice duties (Articles 8/9) starts with just the correction order — add a consent screen and you're compliant. Civil damages? Article 29 incorporates Article 28: where actual damages are hard to prove, NT$500–20,000 per person per incident. NT$20,000 is the ceiling — in a 2023 High Court data-breach case, non-property damages hit exactly NT$20,000, while the plaintiff's nearly NT$100,000 in fraud losses got nothing for "lack of adequate causation." In June 2026, a New Taipei gym's data case saw an NT$880,000 claim fail on the same causation ground. In October 2026, a Consumers' Foundation lawyer publicly advised in the MOS Burger breach: skip the courts, file a complaint — a consumer ombudsman's mediation might even get you store credit.

Compare Texas: per-violation fines with no cap — up to $10,000 each, $250,000 involving seniors 65+. One attorney general's filing is a multi-billion-dollar bargaining chip. Taiwan: per-person, NT$20,000 cap per head, NT$200 million total cap per incident. A manufacturer's maximum exposure is computable on a napkin — which is probably the structural reason nobody in Taiwan has sued.

So the three most realistic paths for a Taiwanese consumer today: first, exercise your data-subject rights under Articles 3 and 11 — write the manufacturer demanding it stop collecting and delete your ACR data (it must respond within 30 days), and turn off ACR manually in the meantime; second, call 1950 or file online with the Executive Yuan's Consumer Protection Committee — the fastest route to state power getting involved; third, report unlawful collection and beyond-purpose use to the competent authority and push for an investigation with a correction order.

And what does "winning" look like in Taiwan? Civilly, a few thousand to NT$20,000 per person. Administratively, a correction order — the Taiwanese version of the Texas settlement: every TV in Taiwan gets a clear opt-in consent screen, plus a NT$50,000–500,000 fine.

In other words: suing over ACR in Taiwan, what you'd win isn't money — it's the consent screen. And that is precisely the most publicly valuable part of the whole affair.

## The Next War

This war has a rehearsal, and the stage was YouTube. In 2023 YouTube began small-scale anti-ad-block tests; by late 2024 the crackdown went global; by mid-2025 it had moved to server-side ad injection — ads stitched directly into the video stream, invisible to traditional blockers. The numbers tell the story: in the month after a tightening, traffic to ad-blocker pages surged 336%, but only 11% of users said they'd use blockers less and only 12% said they'd consider Premium (allaboutcookies.org data, single source, for reference). Consumers would rather escalate than surrender.

So will TV makers simply sell you a "pay to remove ads" option? The answer, verified: no. No TV manufacturer offers a paid ad-free OS tier — Samsung TV Plus explicitly has none. The closest analog is on the content side (YouTube Premium), not the TV OS.

As of October 22, 2026, no manufacturer has officially responded to the debloating wave either. The incident is five days old; they may still be assessing — or still deciding whether this is a PR problem or a business-model problem.

dumbtv's README carries a fine manifesto: "Your TV should do what you paid for: your apps, your HDMI inputs, and nothing else. No ads from the TV itself, no watch tracking, no update you did not ask for."

The other side of that sentence is this article's most honest ending: today, no manufacturer will sell you the "pay for redemption" button. So consumers built one themselves, with AI. When a business model built on ad-subsidized hardware meets an AI that can dismantle the subsidy, nobody has written the next move — the manufacturers included.
