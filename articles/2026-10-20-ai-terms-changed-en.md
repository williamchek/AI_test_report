# The Contract You Clicked "Agree" On Is Not the Same Contract Three Months Later

> A study says 60% of 175 AI tools changed their "can I use the free tier commercially" terms within 15 weeks. The number is real — but it hides some unit games. A second list names nine companies that "train AI on your content by default"; checked closely, two are mislabeled and one is a gray zone. This article unpacks both scary headlines, looks at what the law says, and asks what you can do.

Consider a real case. This June, an AI image tool called Lexica said, in writing, that images made on its free plan could be used commercially. By October, the terms had changed: commercial use now requires a paid plan. There was no big announcement. You did not receive an email titled "Your rights have shrunk." The change was found by a small website whose owner re-read the terms of 175 AI tools by hand — because re-reading is the only way anyone finds out.

This is the new normal of the AI era: the contract under your feet keeps moving. This article answers four questions. How frequent are these changes, really? Is "they train AI on your content" true? Does the law care? And what can an ordinary person do?

## 1. Unpacking that "60%"

ClipJury is a site that reviews AI creative tools. Its method is dumb and rigorous: it archived, word for word, the terms and pricing pages of 175 AI video, voice, music and image tools, set a baseline on June 23, 2026, re-read everything on September 5 and October 3, and diffed every change affecting free-tier monetization rights. The result: **105 tools had changed something — 60%**. The arithmetic checks out. Fifteen tools explicitly tightened free-tier commercial rights — Lexica, PicLumen, and the music generator Suno are on the named list (editor's note: no complete list of the fifteen exists publicly; all we can confirm is what the study itself claims).

But as this study travels, it has picked up things the original never said. Each deserves a line.

**"31 vs 6 moved against creators" — wrong unit.** The 31 and 6 aren't tools; they're *scored factors*. The study grades each tool on seven dimensions, and across two sweeps, 31 factors moved toward "more risk for creators," 6 toward less. So among the factors that moved directionally, unfavorable vs favorable runs about five to one — not "sixty percent of tools are out to get you." Most recorded changes didn't show up in directional factors at all.

**"46% fell to 32%, so changes are slowing" — that's a window trick.** The 46% is a cumulative figure over 74 days; the 32% covers 28 days. Normalize by time and the second sweep was actually *faster*, not slower. Two numbers side by side look like a trend; they're two different rulers.

**"Silently changed" — never measured.** This is the key one. The researcher says so himself on the page: "We do not hold accounts at these tools, so we cannot say how many emailed their users." *Silently* isn't data; it's inference. What can be confirmed is only this: changes lived on web pages, discoverable by re-reading.

**The full list doesn't exist.** The study page says the complete change log lives on the site's changelog page. We opened it on October 20, 2026; it displayed "No terms changes recorded yet." The 105, 41 and 15 cannot be independently recomputed.

Finally, disclosure. ClipJury is run by one man, Abdallah Amjid, who says he runs faceless YouTube channels. Revenue comes from affiliate commissions, terms-audit services at $499–$1,499 a pop, and a $1,499-a-month "terms-change monitoring" retainer. The study's conclusion — changes are frequent, mostly unfavorable, you need continuous monitoring — directly drives the value of what he sells. That doesn't prove the data is fabricated: the methodology keeps exact quotes on file, and he publicly disclosed 18 places where his own early data had cited paid-plan language as evidence about free plans, downgrading and re-doing them all — a point in his favor. But the "problem-seller publishing problem data" structure is something readers deserve to know.

## 2. "Nine companies train on your content by default": actually four different things

The second widely-shared list comes from HasData, a company that sells web-scraping APIs (content marketing too — worth disclosing). It names nine companies that "keep training on user content switched on by default." The list is real, but checked against each company's own terms and help pages, the nine are four different things:

**Actually default-on (most of them).** OpenAI is the textbook case: personal plans (including paid Plus and Pro) may use your conversations for training; you opt out yourself in settings, and **opting out covers only future conversations**. GitHub belongs on the list, with a date: since April 24, 2026, personal Copilot plans train on "interaction data" — your prompts, its suggestions, code snippets. Note: the training covers your *interactions* with Copilot, not the code sitting quietly in your repositories. Microsoft's consumer Copilot is also default-on, with the original text's qualifier "in some markets" — six countries excluded, business accounts excluded entirely. Mistral's chat product (formerly Le Chat, now renamed Vibe) is default-on for personal users, default-off for enterprise. DeepSeek largely checks out, and is the weakest on tiering among the nine: no clear exemption for API use, and the data is stored in China. Stability AI is the extreme case: even the developer-facing Platform API has to be opted out.

**Two that got it wrong.** Adobe tops HasData's ranking as the "worst offender," but Adobe's binding terms say in three separate places that it does not use customers' local or cloud content to train generative AI (except content you submit to the Adobe Stock marketplace). What is actually default-on is something called Content Analytics — using machine learning to analyze files on its servers to improve products. You can turn it off, but that is not "training AI on your artwork." Quora is even more direct: its help page states upfront that "we currently do not use answers, posts or comments added to Quora by our user community to train LLMs used for generating content on Quora" — what's default-on is a *permission switch for the future*: the behavior hasn't happened yet, the permission is already set. You can fairly criticize permission-before-behavior, but writing "currently training on you" is a factual error.

**One gray one.** Anthropic is not quietly default-on. In August 2025 it changed consumer terms: every user gets a pop-up that **forces a choice** — choose, or you can't keep using the product — and the data-sharing toggle inside it is pre-selected *On*, a design the press criticized as a dark pattern. The side effect is retention: consenting users see their chat retention stretched from 30 days to five years. Lumping it with OpenAI's quiet opt-out erases the "forced choice" distinction.

Spread the nine out and two solid conclusions emerge. First, **opt-outs almost universally cover the future only**: OpenAI and Microsoft both say so in their own pages — toggling off excludes later conversations; what's already been used doesn't come back. Second, **the real divide isn't free vs paid, it's which contract you signed**: paid personal plans — ChatGPT Plus/Pro, Claude Pro/Max, Copilot's personal tiers — are still default-on after you pay; what's exempt are Business, Enterprise and API contracts. Google writes it most nakedly: Gemini API's additional terms are split into "Unpaid Services" and "Paid Services" sections — the free section says plainly it may use your inputs and outputs to improve products, that human reviewers may read them, and warns in bold not to submit sensitive information; the paid section says otherwise. The most interesting footnote: users in the EEA, Switzerland and the UK get paid-tier data terms even on the free plan — same free account, different rights in Europe vs Taiwan. Regulation changes the default.

## 3. The rhythm of history: nobody reads it when it changes; it explodes when it's discovered

This isn't new to the AI era. Line up the cases from the last decade and the rhythm is strikingly consistent: **terms change first, get discovered months later, then explode.**

2012 is the ancestor case. Instagram published new terms; one sentence — businesses may pay Instagram to display your username, likeness and photos in sponsored content, with no compensation to you — was read as "Instagram will sell your photos." Co-founder Kevin Systrom clarified, then apologized, delivering a line quoted ever since: "We don't own your photos — you do." The advertising terms were rolled back to the 2010 original.

2023's Zoom was the AI-era version. The terms were actually changed in March: a license letting Zoom use "service-generated data" for "any purpose," including training and tuning AI models. Five quiet months, then a blog post dug it up in August and it exploded. The EFF's Katharine Trendacosta asked AP a question that still has no answer: "If the administrator consents and it's your boss at your work who requires you to use Zoom, how is that really consent?" On August 7, Zoom added "without your consent" to its promise not to use your audio, video or chat for AI training; challenged on the consent loophole, on August 11 it dropped the qualifier and gave an unconditional commitment — but note that behavioral and usage telemetry, "service-generated data," remains outside that promise to this day.

2024's Adobe was the biggest. The contested terms were written in February, giving Adobe the right to "access, view, or listen to your content" by automated and manual means, with automated systems analyzing it via machine learning. In June, a "you can't use the software without re-accepting" forced pop-up marched everyone to the terms page, and creators exploded — including fears about NDA-protected client work. Within 12 days Adobe published two blog posts, and on June 18 shipped new terms writing "we will not use your Local or Cloud Content to train generative AI models" into the binding text. Chief Strategy Officer Scott Belsky: "We've never trained generative AI on customer content, taken ownership of a customer's work, or allowed access to customer content beyond legal requirements." But a user's reply captured the essence: "I give precisely zero f*cks about Adobe's clarifications or blog posts. You forced people to sign new Terms. Legally they are the only thing that matters."

Two counter-cases from the same year. Slack's privacy policy said it analyzed customer data to develop AI/ML models, with opt-out only by the workspace admin emailing in; surfaced, it merely reworded its clarification — "traditional machine learning uses only de-identified aggregate data, doesn't read message content" — changing not a word of policy. GitHub Copilot took the never-retreat path: the dispute moved from the community into the courts and has run four years; this September the Ninth Circuit upheld the dismissal of the DMCA claim, while the contract claim over open-source licenses is still being litigated.

Four endings: write it into the terms (Adobe), delete the qualifier (Zoom), reword without changing policy (Slack), see you in court (Copilot). Which ending happens depends on the volume of the backlash and the company's calculus — rarely on what the terms actually said.

## 4. What the law says: three jurisdictions, same bottleneck

Can a company unilaterally change contract terms? Three jurisdictions give surprisingly aligned answers: **everything comes down to notice and genuine consent.**

The most-cited U.S. case is Douglas (2007). The Ninth Circuit put it bluntly: "Parties to a contract have no obligation to check the terms on a periodic basis to learn whether they have been changed by the other side. Indeed, a party can't unilaterally change the terms of a contract; it must obtain the other party's consent before doing so." Posting new terms on a website without telling users makes the changes unenforceable. In February 2024 the FTC spelled it out: adopting more permissive data practices — say, starting to share data with third parties or training AI on it — and telling consumers only "through a surreptitious, retroactive amendment to its terms of service or privacy policy" "may be unfair or deceptive." The FTC had enforced the same shape of case back in 2004, when its consumer-protection chief left a quotable line: "Changing the rules after the game has been played is unfair."

The EU has two instruments. The 1993 Unfair Terms Directive puts "enabling the seller or supplier to alter the terms of the contract unilaterally without a valid reason which is specified in the contract" on its gray list for case-by-case review — note, not an outright ban: changes with a valid reason, reasonable notice, and the consumer's right to walk away are expressly permitted. The other is the GDPR's consent standard: consent must be a "clear affirmative action," and the European Data Protection Board says "merely proceeding with use of a service" doesn't count. The "consent or pay" model got an EDPB opinion in 2024 finding it mostly fails valid-consent requirements.

Taiwan's Consumer Protection Act has three lines of defense. Articles 13 and 14: terms not disclosed to the consumer, or not reasonably foreseeable, "do not become part of the contract" — in other words, a change that isn't announced doesn't get into the contract. Article 12: terms violating good faith and materially unfair to consumers are void — a case-by-case challenge route. Article 17 is the interesting one: it explicitly authorizes regulators to list "the business retaining the right to change the contract" as a prohibited term; once an industry is so designated, the clause is void — but it's an authorization mechanism, not an automatic blanket ban. We found no landmark Taiwanese court ruling on unilateral changes to online terms of service, and no Consumers' Foundation statement on AI-service terms. This front hasn't opened in Taiwan.

## 5. Self-defense: ratings you can't fully trust, but three things you can do today

What can an ordinary person do? Start with a sobering spot-check. ToS;DR ("Terms of Service; Didn't Read"), the volunteer project that has rated terms A-to-E since 2012, carries the tagline: "I have read and agree to the Terms" is the biggest lie on the web." We checked its grades for mainstream tools on October 20, 2026: **OpenAI: C, Adobe: C, GitHub: D, Claude: D, Canva: D** — not one A or B. And even the ratings need discounting: Canva's entry hasn't been updated since 2023, Claude's D was assigned without a full review.

Here's a vacuum: **consumer-grade terms-change trackers barely exist.** The EFF built TOSBack in 2009 to show terms diffs to the public; it's history now. Services like TermsMonitor track changes today, but for enterprise compliance. What ordinary people can assemble is general-purpose tooling:

**First, preserve evidence.** Use the Wayback Machine's Save Page Now to keep a timestamped snapshot of the terms and pricing pages of tools you depend on. If rights get cut later, you can at least point to "the version I agreed to." The honest caveats: pages behind logins or heavy JavaScript may archive incompletely, and a snapshot is evidentiary support, not notarized proof.

**Second, monitor.** Feed terms pages into a page-change monitor like Visualping (the free tier watches five pages), or self-host the open-source changedetection.io, filtered to alert only on keywords like train, retain, license. It only tells you "the page changed" — a human still has to read whether it got better or worse — but at least you won't be the last to know.

**Third, manage your switches, and know your contract.** Each product's training switch is separate: within one company, GitHub Copilot and consumer Copilot are different policies; Mistral explicitly says its Vibe and API toggles don't talk to each other. Three products, three switches to find. And internalize the real divide: **paid personal plans don't buy you out of training; business contracts do.** If your work is genuinely sensitive (client data, unpublished work, NDA projects), the answer is the business tier or the API — not the most expensive personal plan.

Finally, an honest footnote: this article's Taiwan angle is a blank. Multiple Chinese-language searches found no Taiwanese creator or independent-media worker on record describing harm from an AI tool's terms change — Taiwan has usage scale (a financial regulator survey put AI adoption in finance at 43%) and an active debate about Chinese-language corpus copyright, but the story of "a terms change hit Taiwanese users" doesn't exist in the public record. That may mean Taiwanese users haven't been burned yet, or that they have been and nobody wrote it down. Either way, it awaits the first person willing to say so on the record.

## 6. The follow-up: after the terms changed, did anyone pop a new consent window?

At this point a natural question: after these tools changed their terms, did even one of them pop a serious window saying "terms changed, please re-agree"? We spread out ClipJury's monitored tool list, read each current terms page's "how we notify you of changes" section, and searched for public records of consent windows in 2026. Seventy-one vendors checked, 59 classifiable.

The cleanest possible conclusion first: **zero confirmed "forced re-consent pop-ups for terms changes" in 2026.** The only terms text that describes such a mechanism belongs to InVideo, the Indian video tool: it promises 30 days' advance email and in-product notice for material changes, and says that for paid and European users, material changes "take effect only when you affirmatively accept them, for example by acknowledging an in-product notice on your next login." That's the closest design to genuine consent we've found — but it exists only in terms text, with zero public record of it ever being enforced in 2026.

The terms layer is actually less bleak than you'd think. Of the 59 classified, 31 (53%) promise email or in-product notice for material changes, some with hard numbers: OpenAI commits to at least 30 days' notice for materially adverse changes, Meta at least 30, Google's current terms at least 15 days by email, Fliki 30. The devils are in the conditions: the promises almost always cover only "material" changes, and materiality is mostly defined by the vendor; several promises are "efforts" obligations — HeyGen's terms say it will use "reasonable efforts to *attempt* to notify you": it promises to try, not to notify. Thirteen are grade B: posting-only, continued use equals acceptance — PixVerse expressly reserves the right to change without notice. The other fifteen are vague or say nothing. And even in grade A, the acceptance mechanism is almost universally continued use — the notice gives you a chance to read, not a "disagree" button.

Between "promised" and "done," no evidence connects the two. The study-named PicLumen is grade A on paper — material changes get email or on-site notice. Yet when it removed free-tier commercial rights in 2026, no user has been found reporting any notice. The study-named Suno goes the other way: we couldn't verify its terms (the site blocked our research tools), yet it left the best public record — a public announcement on August 10, 2026 that terms would change September 3, plus an "our terms are changing soon" banner atop the old terms page. That was a notice, not a consent window. The most ironic control case: the one company known to have used a forced re-consent pop-up in history is Adobe (2024) — the very incident that ignited creator fury. The mechanism exists, it's been used; and the industry's 2026 standard move is: send a notice (or don't), keep using, and you're deemed to have agreed.

Twelve couldn't even be classified, and the reasons are themselves a finding: LOVO filed for Chapter 7 liquidation and its site is gone; PlayHT sunset its platform at the end of 2025, so there were no 2026 terms to agree to; Gling, Kaiber, FlexClip and Lumen5 don't even have locatable official terms pages. Tools where you can't even find out how they plan to notify you — that's also part of the answer.

## Conclusion: the only version that won't change is the one you saved

Stack the rulings and guidelines from three jurisdictions and the judges and regulators are really saying one thing: rules can change, but you must notify first, obtain genuine consent, and never apply them retroactively. That sentence still lives in case law and guidance; it hasn't become a product reality where "when terms change, you get a notice in human language." Until then, the one version of your contract that you can be certain won't change is the one you saved yourself.

Save a snapshot on the day you sign up. Spend ten minutes each quarter checking your switches. Unromantic — and the most effective things an ordinary user can do right now.
