# The First Phone at Twelve: We Finally Have the Numbers. The Answer Isn't Written Yet.

> A U.S. study tracked 10,000 children; a Swedish team compared 20,000 twins. Together they produced concrete risk numbers. From Australia's world-first ban to France's constitutional veto to a law Taiwan passed this August, governments are now answering the same question in legislation: at what age should a child get a phone?

At what age do children in Taiwan get their first phone? A survey this year by the Child Welfare League Foundation produced a number that makes parents pause: **9.98 years old** — the average age of first ownership has slipped below ten for the first time, and more than nine in ten junior-high students now carry a phone of their own. Nearly every parent of a school-age child has asked the same question: *how old is old enough?* Until recently, it could only be answered by gut feeling. Over the past year, two heavyweight studies have turned it into numbers, while governments have argued the question all the way from national guidelines to a constitutional court. This article lays out the evidence — including where the evidence doesn't hold — and then looks at what governments and families around the world are actually doing about it.

## 1. Where the numbers come from: the two hardest studies

The first comes from the United States. The Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study followed **10,588 twelve-year-olds**, 63.6% of whom already owned a smartphone. Comparing owners with non-owners, the children with phones had 31% higher odds of depression, 40% higher odds of obesity, and 62% higher odds of insufficient sleep — defined as under nine hours a night. The study was led by Ran Barzilay, a child psychiatrist at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, and published in *Pediatrics*, the journal of the American Academy of Pediatrics.

In the official press release, Barzilay said the results suggest smartphones should be treated "as a significant factor in adolescent health" and that giving a child a phone deserves careful thought. But he also conceded the study's biggest gap, telling CBS: "We didn't even look at what the kids were doing on their phones. We asked one simple question: is merely *owning* your own phone at this age associated with health outcomes?"

That admission matters, because it draws the boundary of what this study can claim: **association, not causation**. Children who have phones and children who don't may come from different kinds of families to begin with. The team ran one cleaner analysis: following the same children from twelve to thirteen, those who acquired a phone in between had 57% higher odds of clinical psychological problems a year later, and 50% higher odds of insufficient sleep. Stronger evidence — still not proof of cause.

The second study comes from Sweden's Karolinska Institutet, published on October 8 in *Nature Human Behaviour*. The team analyzed **21,797 twins** (roughly 10,900 pairs), followed from age nine to twenty-eight. The beauty of a twin design is control: identical twins share their genes and their upbringing, so comparing siblings against each other strips out the two biggest confounders — genetics and family environment.

The findings are striking. Children who at fifteen spent more than six hours a day on "interactive screen time" — gaming, chatting, browsing — had, in the full-sample comparison, **3.37 times** the risk of later being diagnosed with depression or anxiety on weekdays. But switch to within-pair twin comparisons and the number falls to **1.65 — and is no longer statistically significant**. In other words, genes and family environment explain away more than half the association. Corresponding author Tong Gong put it candidly in the institute's release: "The association did not disappear entirely, but it weakened considerably, suggesting that both genetics and parenting play important roles."

The same team offered two practical details. **Passive viewing showed no credible link to mental-health problems** — the issue is interaction, not screens as such. And a follow-up study published this June found that children who got a phone at thirteen fared better than those who got one before twelve, and that **keeping the phone outside the bedroom at night** cut the odds of insufficient sleep by close to 40%.

Co-author Emma Frans's advice to parents: "Future recommendations shouldn't just look at how much time children spend in front of screens. To give good advice, we also need to know what they're doing on those screens, why, and which personal and family factors shape how it affects them."

## 2. Governments: a spectrum from ban to unconstitutional

The numbers are in. Government responses, however, span a spectrum with extremes at both ends.

**Australia went furthest.** Its Online Safety Amendment, passed in late 2024, was a world first: major platforms must take "reasonable steps" to keep Australians under sixteen from holding accounts, with fines of up to nearly A$50 million. It took effect on December 10, 2025, initially covering ten platforms including Facebook, Instagram, TikTok and YouTube. In the first month, officials said 4.7 to 5 million underage accounts were removed or blocked, and polls show 77% of Australians support the law. The gaps showed immediately, too: the government itself has called platform measures inadequate, nearly a third of parents say their children still have accounts, and Reddit has challenged the law in the High Court on free-speech grounds.

**France demonstrated the other ending.** France banned phones in primary and middle schools back in 2018, and recently piloted "hand them in at the door" schemes. This July, parliament passed a ban on social media for under-fifteens — and on August 14 the Constitutional Council struck it down as a disproportionate restriction on freedom of expression, with age verification that lacked privacy safeguards. Same road, two outcomes: Australia paved it with fines; France was stopped by its constitution.

The middle ground has its own calculations. **The Netherlands** relies on a non-binding national guideline, yet compliance has been strikingly high: a government-commissioned evaluation found 75% of secondary schools reporting better student focus — though only 28% reported better grades, and another study found full bans no better than partial ones on wellbeing and bullying measures. **Britain** this year made its guidance statutory: schools must comply in principle, but may deviate for good reason. **South Korea's** story is the most instructive: its 2011 "Cinderella law," which barred under-sixteens from online gaming after midnight, was repealed a decade later because it governed PC games while children had moved to phones; since March this year, phones are banned in classrooms nationwide instead. Regulation moved from midnight to the classroom.

**Taiwan moved this year, too.** The Child Welfare League Foundation's survey sketches the scene: 91.6% of junior-high students have phones, average 38.4 hours online a week, and more than half of parents impose no limits on usage contexts. Yet in the same survey, **77.3% of children oppose an under-sixteen social media ban** — policymakers and children stand on opposite sides. On August 21, the Legislative Yuan passed amendments to the School Health Act, bringing phones under addiction-risk management and giving campus rules their first clear legal basis; the Ministry of Education announced that from the September term, schools will collect and store phones during class hours. A Taoyuan city councilor's proposed local ordinance passed its first reading on October 7 — and was withdrawn by its own sponsor the next day, over unresolved questions of storage liability and damage compensation. At a press conference, Yu Ding-tse, chairman of a New Taipei parents' association, put the parental case this way: "Parents should not be merely controllers; they should be navigators, leading their children to explore the digital world."

## 3. Families: honest tools, and the case for presence over software

Back at home, what cards do parents actually hold?

The best-known is **Wait Until 8th**. In 2017, Brooke Shannon, a Texas mother of three, started a pledge: parents commit to no smartphone until eighth grade (roughly age thirteen or fourteen), and a pledge only activates when ten families from the same school and grade sign up — collective action as the answer to the "but everyone else has one" prisoner's dilemma. The organization says more than 100,000 families have signed (its own figure, not independently verified). Its motto is blunt: childhood is too precious to waste on a smartphone.

Jonathan Haidt, the NYU professor behind *The Anxious Generation*, prescribes four norms: no smartphones before high school, no social media before sixteen, phone-free schools, and far more unsupervised play in the real world.

But the honest answer about parental-control tools may disappoint. Google, in its own Family Link documentation, **admits** its filtering "isn't perfect" and that children may still reach content you'd rather they didn't. Research from the Oxford Internet Institute found no evidence that filtering tools effectively shield children from harmful content — reportedly, in over 99.5% of cases, whether a child encountered such content had nothing to do with whether parents had set filters. A University of Central Florida study cuts deeper: children in families using authoritarian monitoring apps encountered *more* online risk, not less; what correlated with less harm was parents' actual involvement. Researcher Arup Kumar Ghosh: "Parental involvement and direct supervision were both associated with fewer peer problems and less online victimization for teens — and neither had anything to do with whether a monitoring app was used." The most ironic footnote comes from Meta itself: internal research (Project MYST) disclosed in social-media addiction litigation acknowledged that parental controls and household rules had almost no relationship with teenagers' self-reported self-control. (The study was never published; this is per court-disclosure reporting.)

The American Academy of Pediatrics takes a different road: not blocking, but agreements — screen-free zones (the dinner table, desks, bedtime), one screen at a time, autoplay off, and talking with children about advertising and influencer marketing. Tools go obsolete. Agreements are a relationship.

## 4. The other side: don't rush to conclude

An article that presented only one side would be dishonest. The counterattack is strong, and much of it comes from top journals.

Developmental psychologist Candice Odgers, reviewing Haidt's book in *Nature*, argued his causal claims are "not supported by science," and warned that fixating on phones "could distract us from responding to the true causes of the adolescent mental health crisis" — aftershocks of the financial crisis, family economic stress, and caregivers' own mental-health collapse rank higher on her list. She also offered a line worth sitting with: "Caregiver mental health is the strongest predictor of child mental health."

Oxford's Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski were more specific, in a 2019 analysis of three large datasets covering 350,000 people: digital technology use explains **at most 0.4%** of the variation in adolescent wellbeing. "Even if you told me how long a teenager uses digital devices each day," Orben said, "I could barely predict their wellbeing." In their data, incidentally, wearing glasses correlated more negatively with wellbeing than using technology. In 2023, Przybylski's team analyzed data from nearly a million people across 72 countries and concluded there is no evidence that Facebook's spread is associated with psychological harm. The U.S. National Academies' 2023 consensus report set the tone: the existing research shows small effects and weak associations — contrary to the prevailing cultural narrative that social media is broadly harmful to adolescents, the reality is more complicated.

But the other side hasn't won outright, and that needs saying plainly. Re-running the data, Haidt's camp concedes the overall association across all children and all screen types is indeed tiny — but narrowed to *girls plus social media*, the correlation is around 0.20: not large, not zero. The most precise formulation is this: **the overall effect is very small; the association is larger in specific subgroups; and it is still not causation.** Which is exactly why the Swedish twin study matters so much: it demonstrates the phenomenon itself — the stricter the method, the smaller the effect.

## 5. The open-source toolbox: what parents can build themselves

William wanted to know: is the open-source world working on this? It is — on two main fronts.

**The first is network-level control**: filtering at the DNS layer, so the whole household is covered without installing anything on each device. **Pi-hole** (61,000 stars) turns a Raspberry Pi or small home server into a DNS sinkhole that every device's requests pass through — though blocking adult content requires adding blocklists yourself. **AdGuard Home** (37,000 stars) ships with built-in parental controls and search-engine SafeSearch, the friendliest option for parents who don't want to tinker. **Technitium** (10,000 stars) suits Windows households and can apply different rules per child's device. **NextDNS's** command-line client (4,200 stars) can be installed on a router to force all traffic through filtering — with a caveat: only the client is open source; the NextDNS service itself is commercial. **OpenAppFilter** (2,900 stars) goes further: running on an OpenWrt router, it identifies and blocks traffic from over a hundred apps — TikTok, YouTube and the like — which plain DNS filtering cannot do.

**The second front is on-device control, and it splits into two worlds.** First, an important finding: **on iPhone, open source is close to a vacuum.** Apple's Screen Time API requires developers to apply case-by-case for a "Family Controls" distribution entitlement; without it, an app simply won't work once published. The open-source community has produced little beyond teaching demos (the one Traditional Chinese implementation we found, Learnity, has three stars). For iPhone families, the most reliable tools remain Apple's built-in Screen Time with Family Sharing; the only open route is the DNS layer. **Android offers more**: **Zenith** (482 stars, still actively updated on the day we checked) is the most complete open-source digital-wellbeing tool, with bedtime schedules, focus modes and site blocking — but with an honesty disclosure: it is built for self-management, has **no parental remote control**, and a determined child who knows the settings can turn it off. Minimalists can try **ScreenTime** (188 stars), which does exactly one thing: show today's usage in the notification shade. "No judgment, just awareness" — a good fit for families who want to start with a conversation.

## Conclusion: the reminder behind 0.64

After twenty thousand words of source material, the number that stayed with me wasn't any of the dramatic multipliers. It was a quiet one: put the phone outside the bedroom at night, and a child's odds of insufficient sleep fall by nearly 40%. No money, no legislation, no app.

Barzilay says giving a child a phone deserves careful thought. Odgers says don't let the phone debate distract us from the real causes. The two are not in conflict: care and calm can coexist. For parents, perhaps the most practical starting point isn't which tool to buy or which ban to support. It's a conversation tonight about one small thing — at bedtime, the phone sleeps in the living room.
